A critical flaw in a long-dead ASP.NET library is being exploited, and there will never be a patch

Mandiant disclosed on November 18 that a legacy ASP.NET anti-CSRF library called Idunno.AntiCsrf has a critical deserialization flaw, CVE-2026-131914, scored CVSS 9.3. A remote attacker can run code on the web server with a single unauthenticated web request, no login needed, and Mandiant says active exploitation in the wild has been observed. The library was last released in December 2008 and the project is defunct, so no fix exists and none is coming. The only remediation is to remove the library from every application that loads it and replace it with a supported anti-CSRF mechanism. The flaw is not in CISA's actively exploited list.

Why it matters: small businesses running older ASP.NET websites may still load this library without knowing it, since it sits quietly in the site's configuration. If your site runs on ASP.NET WebForms, check its Web.config files and application folders for Idunno.AntiCsrf and remove it. An unpatched flaw that can never be patched, and that attackers are already using, is exactly the kind of thing a routine scan will find.

Sources: The Hacker News · Mandiant advisory

Hackers used GL.iNet's cloud service to plant malware on routers and remote-control KVM devices

GL.iNet confirmed on November 20 that hackers exploited a flaw in its GoodCloud cloud remote management service to install custom malware on some customers' routers and KVM devices. GoodCloud lets owners manage routers remotely and control computers plugged into GL.iNet KVM devices, so an infected KVM can reach the computer attached to it. The malware includes root-level reverse shells that call back to attacker servers, plus a script that disguises itself as a system process, installs SSH keys for remote access, and survives reboots. GL.iNet says it fixed the cloud flaw and blocked the attack path on November 20, so no new devices can be infected that way, but the fix does not clean devices that are already infected. Owners must install the new security firmware for their exact model, even if the device looks clean, and check any computer attached to an infected KVM. GL.iNet has not said how many devices were hit or how the attackers got into its cloud service.

Why it matters: GL.iNet travel routers are popular with remote workers and small shops that need a portable office network, and its KVM devices sit between an admin and the computers they manage. If your shop runs a GL.iNet router with GoodCloud turned on, or a GL.iNet KVM, install the security firmware now and check the device for a process disguised as crond -i /tmp/.log.

Sources: BleepingComputer · The Hacker News · GL.iNet security notice

Hackers stole credit card numbers and CVVs from software checkout provider Cleverbridge

E-commerce platform Cleverbridge warned on November 22 that hackers breached its systems and copied customers' payment card data, including card numbers, expiration dates and CVV security codes. Cleverbridge handles checkout, payments and subscriptions for software and SaaS companies, including brands like Red Hat, SUSE, Tenable, Veeam, Jamf and Parallels. It detected unauthorized activity on November 4 and took systems offline the same day, then began notifying affected customers on November 20. It has not said how the attackers got in, how long they were inside or how many customers were affected. It says the incident was not ransomware, no other personal data such as names or billing details was copied, and it is working with TransUnion's Cyberscout on credit monitoring and fraud assistance for affected customers.

Why it matters: small businesses buy a lot of their software through checkout providers like Cleverbridge, often without ever seeing the name. If you bought software online and a card you use for business purchases was charged through Cleverbridge, watch the statement for unauthorized charges and ask your card issuer about a replacement card.

Sources: BleepingComputer

AI providers were quietly copying customer prompts for months, and OpenRouter just cut three of them off

Security researchers at Dragos spent five months, July through November, sending about two million AI prompts laced with unique canary links and credentials to widely used AI services, then watching where those secrets surfaced. They found attackers actively stealing data from inside three AI inference providers, none of which knew it had been breached until the researchers told it. The planted prompts were processed alongside real customer requests, which were almost certainly copied too. On November 22, OpenRouter, the gateway that routes customer AI requests to third-party providers, said it removed three providers from its platform, stripped 125 endpoints of their Zero Data Retention status and notified affected customers. OpenRouter says it was not itself breached. One provider, Phala, said an attacker took control of an inference server and that requests sent to one DeepSeek model through it between October 21 and November 8 should be treated as exposed.

Why it matters: small businesses and dev shops send AI prompts that often contain real secrets, customer data and internal details, and many picked providers that promised not to keep the data. If your shop uses AI tools through OpenRouter or a cheap inference provider, treat anything you sent as possibly read by a third party and rotate any secret that appeared in a prompt.

Sources: The Hacker News · Dragos

Rackspace cloud breach let hackers read customer Kubernetes secrets

Rackspace warned customers on November 21 that hackers breached its Rackspace Spot cloud platform on November 17 using leaked credentials. The attackers acted as legitimate users in some customer organizations, created unauthorized pods in Kubernetes clusters, read Kubernetes Secrets and listed nodes and volumes. Kubernetes Secrets can hold passwords, tokens and API keys for databases and cloud services. Rackspace says it revoked affected tokens and sessions, removed the unauthorized pods and rotated keys, and told affected customers to reissue API tokens, replace refresh tokens and rotate every credential stored in their cluster Secrets. Service was fully restored on November 21 after an extended outage of the admin console.

Why it matters: small dev shops and businesses that run containerized apps on a cloud platform trust the provider to guard their secrets. If your shop runs anything on Rackspace Spot, rotate the credentials the breach could have reached and check logs back to November 17.

Sources: BleepingComputer

Microsoft says attackers exploited a flaw in Entra Connect Health and stole tenant data

Microsoft told administrators on Friday, November 21, that attackers exploited a flaw in its Entra Connect Health service and got read-only access to tenant data. The exposed information included email addresses of tenant technical contacts and administrator accounts, plus tenant IDs, display names, domain names, license tiers and sync status. Microsoft says no passwords, password hashes, authentication tokens or valid cryptographic keys were exposed. It found the flaw on November 9, deployed a server-side fix on November 19, finished its investigation on November 20 and began notifying affected organizations. Microsoft has not said how many tenants were hit or when the attacks began.

Why it matters: most small businesses run their email and identity on Microsoft 365, and Entra Connect Health watches the on-premises identity systems that sync into it. The stolen admin email addresses and tenant details are exactly what a targeted phishing email needs, so expect more convincing phishing aimed at your admins and make sure privileged accounts use phishing-resistant MFA.

Sources: BleepingComputer

Hijacked GitHub accounts are scanning the web for unpatched Next.js sites and posting the stolen keys in public

Researchers at OpenSourceMalware reported on November 18 on a campaign they call React2STEAL. Attackers are using 45 hijacked GitHub accounts to run automated scans from GitHub's own servers, looking for Next.js websites still open to React2Shell, a critical flaw scored 10.0, and a related Next.js bypass. When a scan finds a vulnerable site, it steals environment files, cloud credentials and AI provider keys, then commits the loot to a public GitHub repository where anyone can read it. Researchers found at least 1,281 victim hostnames across about 1,100 domains, and about 7.4 gigabytes of stolen data sitting in public repos. The campaign was still adding new results every 11 minutes when the research was published. The hijacked accounts overlap with the wfx credential-stealing campaign reported here on November 13.

Why it matters: small dev shops and small businesses that run a Next.js site or build with GitHub Actions are the target here, and the stolen data includes Stripe keys and cloud credentials that can drain money or take over infrastructure. If your shop runs Next.js, make sure it is patched against React2Shell, and if a server was ever unpatched, rotate every secret that server could reach.

Sources: OpenSourceMalware · Security Boulevard

Seven critical PHP flaws can let attackers run code on web servers, and older PHP gets no fix

On Thursday, November 19, the PHP project released fixes for 18 flaws, seven of them rated critical at CVSS 9.2. The worst, CVE-2026-129289, is a request smuggling flaw in PHP-FPM: an unauthenticated attacker can slip a second request into a reused backend connection and run their own PHP code on the server, with no login and no special setup. The other critical flaws sit in session handling and the PHP engine, and several can also lead to code execution. The fixes are in PHP 8.2.36, 8.3.37, 8.4.28, 8.5.13 and 8.6.0. None of the flaws is in CISA's actively exploited list, and no attacks in the wild have been reported.

The catch is that PHP 8.1 and older get no fix at all, and three of the new flaws trace back to gaps in the October PHP2Shell fixes. The November fix also turns session upload progress off by default, so sites that rely on it need to turn it back on after updating.

Why it matters: a huge share of small business websites run on PHP, usually through WordPress on shared hosting, and the shop rarely controls the PHP version itself. If your site runs on PHP, ask your web host whether it has updated to a fixed build, and whether your site is still on PHP 8.1 or older, which will never get this fix.

Sources: BleepingComputer · The Hacker News

Critical NGINX flaws (NGINX2Root) can hand attackers control of web servers, and a proof of concept is already public

On November 18, F5 released out-of-band fixes for five critical remote code execution flaws in NGINX. Researchers call the set NGINX2Root, and F5 rates all five at CVSS v4 9.2. The headline flaw, CVE-2026-123007, is a memory corruption flaw in HTTP/2 flow control. It needs no login, has been present since NGINX 1.9.5 in 2015, and Rapid7 has published a public proof of concept. The other four are use-after-free flaws: CVE-2026-123633 in HTTP/2, CVE-2026-123634 in the DNS resolver, CVE-2026-121810 in HTTP/3, and CVE-2026-113425 in the mail proxy.

The fixes are in NGINX Open Source 1.30.7 (stable) and 1.31.9 (mainline), and in NGINX Plus builds 37.3.0.1 and 37.0.8.1. As of November 19, there is no confirmed exploitation in the wild, and none of the five flaws is in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog.

Why it matters: NGINX sits in front of a huge share of small business websites and apps, about 225 million sites in all, and the main flaw needs only HTTP/2 enabled and no login. If your website, web app, or hosting provider runs NGINX, ask whether it has been updated to a fixed build.

Sources: F5 advisory · NGINX security advisories · The Register

Hackers broke into N-able's email security cloud, knocking out spam filter controls

N-able disclosed on Wednesday, November 18, that unknown attackers got unauthorized access to one of the servers in its Mail Assure and SpamExperts Hosted Cloud, the email security service many MSPs and web hosts resell to small businesses. The company disabled the management portal, APIs, quarantine and logging across the Americas, Europe and Asia-Pacific while it investigates. Core mail flow and spam filtering kept running. N-able says it has found no evidence so far that customer data was compromised, including access to mailboxes, but the investigation is ongoing and it has not said how the attackers got in. The outage first appeared on November 16 and was first blamed on a platform configuration problem before N-able said it was a security incident. N-able says this incident is unrelated to the N-central zero-day attacks earlier in November.

Update, November 20: N-able now says the breach was isolated to a single web server, where the attacker may have reached credentials stored on the platform. N-able took the server offline, rotated its own platform credentials, revoked customer API tokens, invalidated OAuth tokens, and reset passwords for local users. The management portal and API are still disabled while N-able validates the fixes, and mail flow and spam filtering keep running.

Update, November 22: N-able has now disclosed the flaw behind the breach, a critical server-side request forgery vulnerability tracked as CVE-2026-134624 (CVSS 9.3), and says it has fixed it and restored the dashboard and APIs as of November 21. The company says it has found no evidence of exploitation beyond the single server, has brought in an outside forensics team, and will publish a formal root cause analysis within two weeks. Local Cloud operators should confirm their servers are on build 20261121.1943.

Why it matters: lots of small shops get their email spam filtering through an MSP or web host that runs Mail Assure or SpamExperts behind the scenes, so a breach at that provider can leave their mail unguarded or unmanageable. If your shop's email filtering runs through an MSP, ask them whether they use Mail Assure or SpamExperts and whether any admin credentials or API keys need rotating.

Sources: The Hacker News · Cyber Security News · SecurityWeek · N-able incident update

Critical Java flaw (Java4Shell) can hand attackers control of servers, and Java hides inside other products

On November 17, CISA urged organizations to patch a critical flaw in Java right away. The flaw, CVE-2026-114938, nicknamed Java4Shell, sits in the fast Base64 decoder inside the Java virtual machine. Specially crafted input can crash the JVM and let an attacker run code without any login. Researchers have already shown it working against Jira, Jenkins, Confluence and Keycloak, tools small shops and MSPs run every day.

It affects Java 17 (from version 17.0.6 on), 21, 25 and 27, but only on servers with newer chips: Intel Xeon from Ice Lake on, and AMD Zen 4 and later. Standard Java 8 and 11 are not affected, and neither are ARM servers. The out-of-support lines (18 to 20, 22 to 24, 26) carry the same flaw and get no fix. Oracle and OpenJDK shipped fixes on November 17 (builds 17.0.21.1, 21.0.13.1, 25.0.5.1 and 27.0.1.1), and other Java builds, including Amazon Corretto, Azul Zulu and Eclipse Temurin, are shipping theirs on their own schedules. No attacks in the wild have been reported so far, and the flaw is not on CISA's actively exploited list.

Why it matters: Java hides inside other products, appliances and containers, and bundled copies often never show up in a software inventory, so a shop can be exposed without knowing Java is even there. Check anything that might run Java, update to your vendor's fixed build, and if you cannot patch yet, starting the JVM with the -XX:UseAVX=2 flag turns off the vulnerable fast path.

Sources: CISA · Datadog Security Labs

Fake job offers are tricking developers into installing an infostealer

Security firm Stormshield reported on November 16 on a large campaign it calls Trigram, which impersonates about 50 AI, blockchain and logistics brands to trick developers into running malware themselves. Stormshield links the campaign to North Korea-linked groups. The lures are fake job portals, fake conference invitations and fake package tracking pages. One trick shows a fake error, then swaps the clipboard so the command the victim pastes actually downloads the malware. The payload, called OverLord, steals browser passwords, cookies, crypto wallets, SSH keys and .env files on Windows, macOS and Linux. The campaign also used a forged commit in a GitHub repository and a fake VS Code and Cursor extension.

Why it matters: small dev shops hire through the same job boards and use the same tools these lures imitate, and one developer pasting a command can hand over the shop's cloud keys and code credentials. Treat any setup step that asks you to paste a command from a website as a red flag, and never run a command copied from a job posting or conference invite.

Sources: The Hacker News · Daily Security Review

Attackers slipped a credential stealer into Microsoft's agent-framework package on PyPI

On Tuesday, November 17, attackers published two malicious versions of Microsoft's agent-framework package to PyPI, versions 1.26.1 and 1.26.2, about 33 minutes apart. The package is Microsoft's Python toolkit for building AI agents and gets about 190,000 downloads a month. Both versions install a startup hook that runs a credential stealer every time Python starts, even if the code never imports the package. The stealer collects Azure and Azure DevOps credentials, GitHub Actions tokens, cloud identity tokens, and credential files such as .env files and .git-credentials, and sends them to an attacker-controlled webhook. PyPI removed both versions within about 45 minutes, and version 1.26.0 is the last clean release.

Why it matters: small dev shops and MSPs that build with Python or run CI pipelines can have cloud credentials stolen by a routine package update. Anyone who installed agent-framework on November 17 should check for versions 1.26.1 or 1.26.2, pin to 1.26.0, rebuild the environment, and rotate every secret that environment could reach.

Sources: The Hacker News · Wiz

Unpatched camera software let cryptominers into 13 organizations

Attackers used GeoVision camera management software to break into Windows machines at 13 organizations and install XMRig cryptocurrency miners, security firm Huntress warned. Most of the intrusions happened between November 8 and 10. The affected machines ran an old build of GV-VMS, version 17.4.8.0 from April 2024, which is exposed to unauthenticated remote code execution flaws in its WebCam Server, while GeoVision has published fixed releases that close those flaws. The loader the attackers installed can also fetch other payloads, so the same foothold could be used for worse.

Why it matters: small shops run IP cameras with software installed by a security integrator, and that server often sits outside the normal patch cycle. If your shop has cameras, find out who manages the camera software and whether it is on a supported, patched release, and make sure its web interface is not exposed to the internet.

Sources: Help Net Security · eSecurity Planet

Optimizely supply chain attack put card-stealing code on 178 organizations' websites

On Monday, November 16, attackers exploited a flaw in Optimizely's Web Experimentation account signup flow and injected malicious JavaScript into customer projects. The poisoned scripts loaded on the websites of 178 organizations, including Zoom, CNN, BBC, Fox News, Salesforce and GitLab, and served visitors code that stole payment card details entered in forms, swapped cryptocurrency wallet addresses, and tried to load exploit kits on iPhones and iPads. Security researcher Scott Helme of Report URI found 188 infected scripts after checking 1,611. Optimizely confirmed the attack, removed the injected code, blocked the vulnerable path and notified affected customers, and by late Monday nearly all infected scripts were clean.

Update, November 18: Optimizely has published an initial findings report on the attack. Helme found the injected payload was rebuilt 26 times during the day, and the code also stole cookies and browser screenshots, not just card details and crypto wallet addresses.

Why it matters: small businesses load third-party scripts on their own websites too, for analytics, chat, booking and A/B testing, and a compromise at the script provider hits every site that loads it. If your site uses Optimizely or any third-party script, ask your web provider whether your scripts were affected, and check card statements and any crypto payments made on Monday.

Sources: BleepingComputer

Retadup, a USB worm thought dead since 2019, is spreading again and dropping CoffeeLoader malware

Retadup, a worm French police took down in 2019, is spreading again through infected USB drives. Someone re-registered its old command domains in 2024, and the worm now downloads CoffeeLoader, a modern loader that can install more malware once it is on a machine. Kaspersky blocked about 15,000 infection attempts in the first nine months of 2026, mostly in Peru, Angola, Russia and Bolivia.

Why it matters: small shops pass USB sticks between machines and plug in old drives found in a drawer, and that is exactly how this worm moves. Once it is in, the loader can pull down password stealers or ransomware, so treat any old flash drive as suspect until it is scanned.

Sources: The Hacker News · Kaspersky · GBHackers

Cursor breach: stolen Fireworks AI key let attackers push a trojanized Claude Code extension

Cursor, the AI code editor, confirmed a breach that let attackers push trojanized copies of Anthropic's Claude Code extension through its marketplace on October 29. The bad versions (2.1.312 and 2.1.313) were live for about 11 hours and may have reached up to 5,000 developers, and they tried to install a persistent SSH backdoor. The break-in started with an access key Cursor had issued to AI vendor Fireworks AI, which was exposed in Fireworks' own breach. The same Fireworks incident also exposed Notion AI transcripts and Uber driver license data.

Update, November 17: Uber has since confirmed the same Fireworks breach exposed driver license images and details for 149,346 drivers, and it began notifying them on November 10.

Why it matters: a lot of small dev shops and solo builders use Cursor and AI coding tools, so a poisoned update from a trusted marketplace is exactly the kind of thing that slips past a small team. Anyone who updated the Claude Code extension in Cursor on October 29 should follow Cursor's remediation steps and rotate any API keys that may have sat in old coding session traces. One vendor's leaked key can cascade into several products at once.

Sources: BleepingComputer · The Hacker News · TechCrunch on the Uber driver data

Police seized malware servers holding 6.4 million stolen logins, but the passwords still work until changed

Europol announced on November 12 and the US Justice Department on November 13 that police in nine countries seized 235 servers and 257 domains behind Gootloader, CastleRAT and StealC. Investigators identified about 6.4 million stolen logins belonging to more than 40,000 victims. Seizing the servers does not invalidate the stolen passwords, they still work until they are changed.

Why it matters: if any staff logins were in that pile, criminals who copied them before the takedown can still use them. Check Have I Been Pwned, reset any reused passwords, and make sure MFA is on.

Source: The Hacker News

A new N-central zero-day was exploited within hours, hitting 21 servers, most already fully patched

A researcher published a proof of concept early on November 12 for a new N-central exploit chain, and attackers used it within hours to compromise at least 21 production N-central servers and plant Monero miners on them. 19 of the 21 were already running the latest Hotfix 2, so patching alone does not stop it. The attack needs the Legacy API enabled and exposed to the internet, and there was no fix released as of November 13.

Update, November 17: N-able released Hotfix 3 on November 16, a critical update that permanently blocks the Legacy API and patches 17 more flaws. Hosted servers are already updated. On-premises customers should install Hotfix 3 now and upgrade all Agents and Probes.

Why it matters: lots of small shops are managed through an MSP that runs N-central, so a hit on the MSP server is a hit on the management platform itself. If your shop uses an MSP, ask them whether the Legacy API is disabled on any internet-facing N-central server.

Sources: BleepingComputer · SOCRadar · SecurityWeek

A breach at Notion's AI vendor exposed Notion AI chat transcripts

Notion says a breach at its AI vendor Fireworks AI exposed Notion AI chat transcripts. Attackers broke into Fireworks AI, which ran some of Notion's AI models, and reached transcripts Notion had sent to Fireworks for a usage feature called Insights. Notion says fewer than 1% of workspaces are affected, most transcripts carry no name or workspace label, and affected customers have been contacted directly. Notion has cut off Fireworks, disabled Insights, and revoked API keys it found inside the exposed chats.

Why it matters: lots of small businesses run their docs and notes in Notion, and anything pasted into an AI chat, like a password or API key, could have been exposed. If Notion contacted you, rotate anything you pasted into Notion AI, and treat AI chats as a place never to paste secrets.

Sources: Notion security advisory · AI Cyber Brief

Hijacked GitHub accounts planted a credential stealer in 1,800+ repositories

Starting Tuesday, November 10, an attacker used 4,837 hijacked GitHub accounts to plant a secret-stealing script in repositories those accounts could write to. The stealer ran in at least 1,806 repositories and harvested GitHub Actions secrets, cloud credentials, and AI provider keys, and it can plant persistence in AWS and Azure that survives a normal secret rotation. The tell is a probe pull request titled "docs: dependency audit notes" from a branch called "wfx-docs".

Why it matters: small dev shops and MSPs that build or manage code on GitHub could have cloud credentials stolen without knowing it. If your shop uses GitHub Actions, check for that pull request title and rotate any secrets and cloud keys the affected repos could reach.

Sources: The Hacker News · Safeguard

Attackers hijacked MSPs' ScreenConnect tools to plant rogue remote access

Attackers broke into at least six managed service providers' cloud-hosted ScreenConnect environments starting Wednesday, November 11, and used the tool's own Run Command feature to install rogue ScreenConnect clients on at least 288 endpoints across 48 organizations. The rogue clients report to attacker-controlled servers, so the access survives even if the MSP resets passwords. ConnectWise confirmed the unauthorized access and disabled five third-party extensions as a precaution. Researchers also found a worm-like toolkit called SCKit that tries to spread to technicians' computers when they connect to an infected machine.

Update, November 17: ConnectWise said its investigation is complete, but attacks continue against on-premises systems where the extensions are still enabled and ScreenConnect is not updated. Technician clients must be on version 26.6.5 or later, which fixes a critical flaw, CVE-2026-84869, that the toolkit uses to spread to technicians' computers. Huntress saw another spike on November 13 affecting 205 hosts across 41 organizations.

Update, November 23: Huntress has now detailed how the attacks work. Between October 20 and November 16, attackers sprayed at least 688,000 fake guest file-transfer requests across 42 MSP ScreenConnect servers, creating 2,408 unauthorized sessions since November 3. On November 12, one technician's workstation was fully compromised in about nine seconds after joining a blank-named session: a guest-pushed batch file downloaded an installer and started a rogue ScreenConnect client pointing at the attacker's own server. The flaw is CVE-2026-84869, fixed in client version 26.6.5.

Why it matters: most small shops get IT support through an MSP, so an attack on the MSP lands directly on the small business. If your shop uses an MSP, ask them whether they use ScreenConnect and whether they have checked for rogue clients.
